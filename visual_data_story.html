What Does It Cost to Eat? A Meal Logging Story

About this data

Over a few days in late March 2026, I logged every meal, snack and drink I had using a Google Form. For each entry I recorded the meal type, where the food came from, how much it cost in NZD, a personal rating out of 5, how long it took to eat, and whether I was alone or with others. The entries go from a $3 home breakfast all the way to a $350 restaurant dinner, which is a pretty wild range for just a couple of days.

What does the average meal actually cost?

Dinner is way more expensive than everything else on average, mainly because of two big group restaurant meals that cost $125 and $350. Lunch and breakfast both sit around the $20 to $30 range, while snacks and drinks are pretty cheap overall.

Bar chart showing average meal cost by type in NZD. Dinner has the highest average at around $130, followed by breakfast, lunch, snack, and drink.

The chart really shows how much a couple of big dinners can throw off the average. Dinner does not even show up that often in the data but those two expensive nights out push it way above everything else.

Does eating with others change how long you take?

Meals with other people tend to take a lot longer than eating solo. The median is higher for group meals and the range is way wider, with one dinner clocking in at 140 minutes. Solo meals on the other hand are usually pretty quick, with a bunch of entries under 10 minutes.

Box plot comparing meal duration in minutes for meals eaten alone versus with others. The with-others group has a higher median and a wider spread including a 140-minute outlier.

That makes a lot of sense really. Eating with others is more of a social thing than just getting food in you. The solo meals are quick and to the point, the group ones turn into proper occasions.

Does spending more mean a better meal?

There is actually no real pattern between how much you spend and how good the meal is. Some of the cheapest meals got the highest ratings, and the expensive ones were all over the place. Each dot is one logged meal, coloured by type, with the size showing how long it took to eat.

Scatter plot with cost in NZD on the x-axis and rating out of 5 on the y-axis. Each point is one logged meal, coloured by meal type and sized by eating duration. No clear positive relationship between cost and rating is visible.

The $350 dinner is way out on the right but it is not even the highest rated meal. Plenty of cheap breakfasts and lunches scored a perfect 5. Spending more really does not mean you will enjoy it more, it is way more about the context.

The takeaway

Three things really stood out from this data:

  1. Dinner gets expensive fast, especially when you are eating out with a group. One big night can completely throw off your averages.
  2. Eating with others takes way longer, and that extra time is a good thing, not a bad one.
  3. Spending more does not mean eating better. Cheap meals rated just as highly as expensive ones, sometimes even higher.

Honestly the big takeaway is that it is about context, not cost. Who you are with and how much time you take matters a lot more than the price tag.
