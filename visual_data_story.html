What Does It Cost to Eat? A Meal Logging Story

About this data

Over two days in late March 2026, I logged every meal, snack and drink I consumed using a Google Form. For each entry I recorded the meal type, where the food came from, how much it cost in NZD, a personal rating out of 5, how long it took to eat, and whether I ate alone or with others. The 20 entries range from a $3 home breakfast to a $350 restaurant dinner — a surprisingly wide spread for just two days.

What does the average meal actually cost?

Dinner is by far the most expensive meal type on average — driven by two large group restaurant meals costing $125 and $350. Lunch and breakfast cluster around the $20–$30 range, while snacks and drinks are the cheapest.

Bar chart showing average meal cost by type in NZD. Dinner has the highest average at around $130, followed by breakfast, lunch, snack, and drink.

The chart shows just how much group dinners out can distort spending — even though dinner appears the least often in the dataset, a couple of expensive entries push its average well above every other meal type.

Does eating with others change how long you take?

Meals eaten with others tend to take noticeably longer than solo meals. The median duration for group meals is higher and the spread is much wider — including a 140-minute dinner — while eating alone skews short, with several entries under 10 minutes.

Box plot comparing meal duration in minutes for meals eaten alone versus with others. The with-others group has a higher median and a wider spread including a 140-minute outlier.

This makes sense: eating with others is a social event, not just fuel. The data captures that difference clearly — the solo meals are quick and functional, the group meals are drawn-out occasions.

Does spending more actually mean a better meal?

Not really. Looking at cost against rating across both logging days, there is no clear relationship. A $3 home lunch scored a perfect 5, while some mid-range restaurant meals scored 3 or lower.

Scatter plot of cost in NZD on the x-axis versus rating out of 5 on the y-axis. Points are coloured by meal type and sized by duration in minutes. The plot is split into two panels by logging date — 30 March and 31 March 2026. There is no clear upward trend between cost and rating.

What seems to matter more is context — who you eat with and how much time you give the meal. The $350 dinner did get a 5, but so did a $4 home lunch. Price alone does not predict satisfaction.

The takeaway

Three things stand out from this data:

  1. Dinner is expensive — especially when eating out with a group. One big night out can dominate the average.
  2. Eating with others takes longer — and that extra time is associated with enjoyment, not inconvenience.
  3. Price does not equal quality — the highest ratings appear at all price points.

The real story here is about context, not cost. Who you eat with and the pace of the meal matters more than how much you spend.
